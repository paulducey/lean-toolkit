**User:** I'm a new charge nurse and my director wants me to start doing gemba walks on the unit. I've never done one. What do I actually do?

**Assistant:** Tell me where you're walking and why — or what you just saw — and I'll build the plan or run the debrief. Since this is your first one: the single most important rule is that a gemba walk is a learning discipline, not an inspection. If you walk the unit to find problems and fix them on the spot, clinicians learn to hide problems or only show you the polished version, and the walk becomes theater. Go to understand. Ask questions. Thank people for what you show you, good and bad.

**User:** How is this different from what my director already does when she does rounds?

**Assistant:** That depends on how she rounds — if she's checking on patients directly, that's clinical rounding, a different activity with a different purpose. If she's walking the unit to observe how the work itself is organized and flowing, and she's fixing things as she spots them, that's the inspection-mode failure this skill exists to prevent. The test either way: does she leave with questions and things she learned, or with a list of corrections handed to staff? That's the line between a gemba walk and rounding-as-inspection.

**User:** Okay. What am I supposed to be looking at?

**Assistant:** Walk the unit in the order patients actually flow through it — admission, assessment, care delivery, discharge prep — not by walking room to room in physical order. At each stop, keep five lenses active at once rather than doing five separate walks:

- **Safety** — anything that could hurt a patient or a clinician right now. This is the one lens where you act immediately if you see something live — a spill, a bed rail down with a fall-risk patient, a blocked crash cart path. Everything else on this list, you observe and ask, you don't fix on the spot.
- **Quality** — where are errors caught, and where might they be getting missed? Look for informal double-checks, workarounds clinicians have built because something isn't working.
- **Flow** — where does care get stuck? Boarding patients, waiting on results, waiting on a bed.
- **Standard work** — is the unit doing things the way its own protocols say? Are the protocols even current?
- **Engagement** — do clinicians know how the unit is doing today, and do they feel heard when they report a problem?

Ask open questions: "Can you walk me through how you do that?" "What makes this step hard?" Avoid anything that sounds like "why didn't you follow the protocol" — that's accusatory and clinicians will stop telling you the truth.

**User:** Just draft it — give me something I can literally carry with me tomorrow morning.

**Assistant:** Here's a pre-walk plan you can carry. I don't know your unit's actual layout or current pain points, so I've marked where you'll need to fill in specifics.

## Pre-Walk Plan — [NEEDS GEMBA: unit name]

**Route (patient-flow order):** Admission/triage → assessment bays → patient rooms in acuity order → discharge prep area. [ASSUMED — verify: confirm this matches how patients actually move through your unit]

**Open questions to carry (one or two per lens, ask them naturally, not as a checklist):**

*Safety:* "Has anything come close to a fall or a med error recently that didn't get reported?"
*Quality:* "When you catch something before it reaches the patient, where does that get written down, if anywhere?"
*Flow:* "What's the thing that most often makes a patient wait longer than they should?"
*Standard work:* "Is there a step in your shift that the written protocol doesn't match what you actually do?"
*Engagement:* "Do you know how today's huddle numbers looked before you clocked in?"

**Debrief reminder — do this within 30 minutes of finishing the walk, before you lose the details:**
1. What did you observe — facts only, no interpretation yet
2. What surprised you
3. What did you learn that you didn't know before
4. What questions does this raise that need more looking or asking
5. What needs immediate action — safety first
6. What goes to the unit's huddle board as an open item, versus what's a bigger PDSA the team should run

**One coaching note for your first walk specifically:** if all you come back with is a list of things to correct, that's a sign the walk slipped into inspection mode. Make sure at least one thing on your debrief is something you learned, not something you'd fix.

**User:** What if I see something bad while I'm actually walking — like an actual hazard?

**Assistant:** That's the one exception to "observe, don't solve." A live hazard — a fall risk, a medication error about to happen, a blocked emergency path — gets handled immediately, right there. Everything else waits for the debrief. Don't confuse "I noticed something concerning" with "this needs action in the next sixty seconds" — most of what you'll see is the first kind.

**User:** My director also wants me to bring back something for the huddle board every time. Is that okay, or does that turn it into an inspection?

**Assistant:** It's fine as long as what goes on the huddle board comes out of the full debrief, not out of a running list you kept during the walk itself. The distinction matters: if clinicians see you jotting corrections as you go, it feels like an inspection in progress even if you never say a word. If instead you walk with open questions, debrief afterward, and only then decide what's board-worthy, the huddle board becomes an output of learning rather than the point of the walk.

**User:** One more — what do I do if a clinician just gives me a one-word answer and doesn't want to talk?

**Assistant:** Don't chase it with more questions in the moment — that's the interrogation trap, and it especially shows up on a first walk when you're eager to get something out of every stop. Thank them, move to the next part of the route, and revisit that relationship over repeated walks. Gemba is a discipline built over many walks, not proven or disproven on the first one.
