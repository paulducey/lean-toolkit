SLIDE 07 A skill is standard work for the assistant

7:15 → 9:45

Now the mechanism, and this is the part I most want you to leave with, because it's your expertise, not mine.

On the left is what's called a "skill." Strip away the word — it's a plain text file. It tells the assistant how to do one job: when to use it, when not to use it, the steps, and what a good result looks like. Look at the headings. "When to use, when not to." "How this skill works." "Quality bar." Now look at the right-hand side. Important steps. Key points. Reasons.

A skill is a job breakdown sheet. The trainee happens to be software.

Everything you know about writing a good JBS applies. Plain language. The reason behind every key point. What "done right" looks like, written down. Notice this one even routes away from itself — it says "OEE isn't the tool for lead time, go use this other one." That's what a good JBS does: it tells you when to stop and get the supervisor.

And here's the thing that should make this room sit up. You already know how to write these. You've been writing standard work for twenty years. Most of the AI industry is just now discovering that the way to make these tools reliable is to write them standard work — and they're calling it "prompt engineering." You have a head start.

If someone asks "do I have to write these?" — "No. Seventeen exist, they're free, you'll get the link. But the ones you write for your own plant will be better than mine."

SLIDE 08 Jidoka for information

9:45 → 11:45

Five design principles went into these, and they're all principles you'd recognize. First: jidoka for information.

The biggest real danger with these tools is not that they're stupid. It's that they're confident. They will hand you a number with no idea where it came from, in a tone that sounds like it came from the MES. So the rule is: every number in every deliverable carries a source tag. Observed — you saw it, you counted it, you timed it. From system — MES, historian, maintenance log. User estimate — somebody's best guess, labeled as a guess. And the fourth one, in yellow.

[NEEDS GEMBA]. Nobody knows this yet. Go look.

That tag is the andon. The assistant pulled the cord. It stopped the line rather than pass a defect downstream. Now you go. And if a number doesn't have a tag, don't trust it — ask where it came from. That's the whole discipline, and it's the same discipline you already apply to a number on a whiteboard.

SLIDE 09 Three more, fast

11:45 → 13:45

Three more, one breath each.

Poka-yoke for data. Missing column in your export? It tells you which one — it never substitutes a value. A downtime reason code it can't map? It goes in an explicit row called Unmapped, with the raw text, instead of being quietly force-fit into "minor stops." And if the math comes out over a hundred percent OEE — it flags it instead of printing it, because that almost always means your "ideal" cycle time is an average, not the best demonstrated rate. A spreadsheet will happily compute a hundred and four percent OEE. This won't.

Observe, don't solve. The gemba-walk skill is taught the same discipline you'd teach a new plant manager. It will ask "what makes this step difficult?" It will not ask "why aren't you following the standard?" It will not hand you a punch list — because a walk that only produces corrections is an inspection, and we wrote that in.

Sensei first, draft on demand. The coaching skills ask a few questions before they draft — left to right across the A3, the way you were coached. But it's Friday, the steering committee is Monday — say "just draft it" and you get a draft in seconds, with every fact it doesn't have marked in yellow instead of invented. The most valuable thing it produces isn't the draft. It's the list of what it doesn't know. That list is where your gemba time goes next.

SLIDE 10 Respect for people, written in

13:45 → 15:15

Slow down. This is the conference theme. Read the headcount line exactly as written.

Fifth principle, and it's the one this conference is about. Respect for people — written into the standard work, not left to good intentions.

Ask one of these tools "how many people can we cut" and here's what happens. It answers the process question. And then it says, once, plainly: "These tools free capacity. What the organization does with freed capacity is a leadership decision." No lecture. No refusal. But it will not do that math for you as if it were neutral.

Lean carries a forty-year scar from being used as a headcount tool. If we hand AI to the same people with the same incentives and no guardrails, we get the same scar, faster. So the guardrail is in the standard work. It's not a setting. It's a sentence the assistant is required to say.

Two more, quickly. Before anyone pastes a document in, it says: leave out the names of employees or patients — roles and initials are fine. Every time, every setting. And no AI jargon. Ever. It says "what you gave me." It says "I made that up — check it." If you say PDSA, it says PDSA. If you're in a hospital, it says unit and clinician. It never corrects you.

SLIDE 13 Where it goes wrong — and how to start anyway

20:00 → 21:45

Let me be honest about where this goes wrong, because a room of experts should trust the speaker who names the failure modes first.

It goes wrong when the practitioner stops going. This tool amplifies the person who goes and sees. It hollows out the person who doesn't. Same tool, opposite outcomes — and the difference is a habit, not a feature. It goes wrong when you read the tone instead of the tags — it will always sound confident; confidence is not a source. And it goes wrong when confidential data goes into a chat box. Know your company's policy before the first paste. The data-safety line is a reminder, not a control.

So how do you start? The way you'd start anything. One practitioner — and it has to be someone who already goes to the gemba. Never the one who doesn't; you'll just get more documents. One skill. One live problem, not a pilot. Two weeks. And one measure — not "did we use AI." Did hours at the gemba go up? If yes, write your own skill for your own plant's document. If no, stop. It's a clipboard, not a strategy.

And I'll be straight with you about my own PDCA: I'm a few weeks in. I can tell you it's cut my document time. I can't yet show you the gemba-hours number across a team — that's the check I'm running now, and I'll tell you what I find.

The questions you'll get

"Which AI? Does this only work with Claude?"The skills are plain-text standard work; they work with any assistant that reads a file. I use Claude Code because it runs the scripts, and I wrote them there. If your company has standardized on something else, the skill files still carry the discipline — the source tags, the closing block — into whatever tool you're allowed to use.
"What about hallucinations?"Slide 8. Every number carries a tag; a number without one doesn't exist in these deliverables. And the scripts do the arithmetic — the model never computes OEE in its head, it runs the calculator and shows the formula. The place it can still be wrong is interpretation, which is exactly the place you'd check a new Black Belt too.
"Isn't this just ChatGPT with a prompt?"The model is a commodity. The standard work isn't. The difference between a prompt and a skill is the difference between telling a new hire "go do OEE" and handing them a job breakdown sheet with the quality bar written down. One of those produces the same result on Tuesday as on Monday.
"Data security — our IT will never allow this."Ask IT which tools are approved and where the data goes; that's the right question and I can't answer it for your company. What I can say: nothing in the skills requires customer or employee names, the data-safety line is built in, and the toolkit works on a de-identified export just as well as a raw one.
"What happens to the operators' jobs?"Nothing in this toolkit touches an operator's job. It touches the CI leader's typing. And slide 10 is the answer to the harder version of that question: the tool frees capacity and says out loud that what you do with it is a leadership decision — the same thing a good sensei would say.
"Does it work in healthcare?"Yes. The skills mirror your vocabulary — unit, clinician, turnaround, PDSA — and carry the protected-health-information warning before any paste. The OEE skill has an MRI-utilization version with an honest note that OEE terms map imperfectly onto a hospital.
"How much does it cost?"The skills are free. The assistant is whatever your company already pays for one. There is nothing to buy from me to use anything I showed today.
"Does it replace the CI department?"It replaces the CI department's typing.
